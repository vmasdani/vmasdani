# Solve the Problem, Not the Panic

Most of our lives, we see problems being solved incorrectly.

Not because people are stupid.

Often, the problem is that **the solution arrives before the problem has been properly understood**.

We see something getting worse. We panic. We look for something that feels like an answer.

And once everyone around us starts doing the same thing, it becomes even harder to stop.

Sometimes the best thing you can do is simply:

> **Slow down. Is this actually the solution?**

## The gasoline example

Take the recent discussion around rising gasoline prices in Indonesia.

When fuel prices rise, people naturally start looking for ways to reduce their fuel expenses. One popular answer is:

> "Switch to an electric vehicle."

There is nothing inherently wrong with that answer.

But there is a difference between **"EVs are a good technology"** and **"buying an EV today is the best response to this particular price increase."**

Imagine you have a gasoline scooter worth Rp10 million.

You replace it with an electric motorcycle costing Rp20 million.

You have immediately spent an additional **Rp10 million**.

Yes, the electric motorcycle may eventually save money on energy and maintenance.

But how long until you recover that Rp10 million?

And what happens if gasoline prices fall again?

That Rp10 million could have paid for a very large amount of gasoline.

The important question isn't:

> "Will an electric motorcycle save money eventually?"

The question is:

> **"Does buying this electric motorcycle solve my actual problem at an acceptable cost and within a reasonable time?"**

Those are different questions.

## EVs have very good arguments

I am not anti-EV.

Quite the opposite.

There are several strong reasons to move toward electric transportation.

### Lower emissions

Electric vehicles can have lower emissions per kilometer than gasoline vehicles, even when the electricity comes partly from fossil fuels. The exact advantage depends heavily on how the electricity is generated and how the vehicle is used. Research on electric motorcycles in Indonesia has found substantial energy and CO2 advantages under tested conditions.

And there is an important long-term argument here.

If Indonesia eventually moves toward much cleaner electricity—nuclear, hydro, geothermal, solar, or other low-carbon generation—the electric vehicle gets cleaner automatically.

A gasoline engine cannot make the same transition.

The fuel itself remains the problem.

### Less dependence on fossil fuels

Electricity can be generated from many sources.

That gives us more flexibility than depending almost entirely on liquid fossil fuels for transportation.

### A chance for Indonesian manufacturers

Electric motorcycles also create an interesting opportunity for local industry.

Their mechanical architecture is comparatively simple: electric motor, controller, battery, chassis, and supporting electronics.

That can create more opportunities for local manufacturing.

This connects to **TKDN — Tingkat Komponen Dalam Negeri**, Indonesia's local-content framework. A higher TKDN means a greater proportion of a product's value or components can come from domestic industry.

That matters beyond the vehicle itself.

A domestic EV industry can create local suppliers, repair ecosystems, engineering expertise, and manufacturing capability.

So yes, there are very good reasons to move toward EVs.

But those reasons do not automatically mean:

> **"Buy one right now because gasoline became more expensive."**

## The price problem

This is where I think people need to slow down.

If the only reason you're buying an EV is:

> "Gasoline is expensive, so I need to save money."

then you have to calculate the economics.

Purchase price.

Trade-in loss.

Financing.

Electricity.

Battery degradation.

Maintenance.

Insurance.

Resale value.

Expected annual mileage.

Expected gasoline price.

Expected electricity price.

And, most importantly:

**break-even time.**

For many buyers, the economics are not an obvious slam dunk.

At best, the purchase can be competitive.

Sometimes it is better.

Sometimes it isn't.

But panic is a terrible financial calculator.

## This pattern happens everywhere

The EV example is just one instance of a much larger problem.

We see:

**Problem → fashionable solution → justification**

instead of:

**Problem → measurement → alternatives → trade-offs → solution**

The first approach feels fast.

The second approach is usually better.

This is why I am skeptical whenever someone presents a complex technology as the automatic answer to a simple problem.

Need more reliability?

> Kubernetes.

Need cheaper computing?

> Serverless.

Need better productivity?

> AI.

Need lower fuel costs?

> Buy an EV.

Sometimes those answers are correct.

Sometimes they're completely unnecessary.

## Sometimes the problem is fear

The hardest part is that we don't always have the luxury of thinking calmly.

Fear changes how we make decisions.

FOMO changes it too.

So do social pressure, pride, confirmation bias, and sunk costs.

We see everyone else buying something and worry that we're falling behind.

We make a decision.

Then we start defending the decision instead of evaluating it.

That is dangerous.

## Cognitive dissonance and sunk cost

Two particularly dangerous patterns are **cognitive dissonance** and the **sunk cost fallacy**.

Cognitive dissonance makes it uncomfortable to admit:

> "I might have made the wrong decision."

So we start looking for evidence that proves we were right.

The sunk cost fallacy makes us think:

> "We've already spent so much, so we have to continue."

But money already spent is gone.

The relevant question is:

> **"Knowing what I know now, would I choose to continue?"**

That question is much harder to answer honestly.

## Look at big systems too

The same thinking applies to governments and large organizations.

Take Indonesia's **Makan Bergizi Gratis (MBG)** program.

The program has a legitimate goal: improving nutrition.

But implementation has experienced serious problems. In early September 2026, roughly 1,950 people were reported sick in suspected food-poisoning outbreaks in several regions in just a few days. Official and media reporting has also documented a much larger cumulative number of cases since the program began.

That doesn't automatically prove the program itself is a bad idea.

It does prove that **the implementation has a serious problem that cannot simply be ignored**.

My suspicion is that large programs can become trapped by the same human biases as individuals.

Once enormous amounts of money, political capital, public promises, and personal reputation have been invested, admitting:

> "Maybe we designed this incorrectly."

becomes psychologically and politically expensive.

There can be pride.

There can be sunk costs.

There can be fear of embarrassment.

There can be pressure to defend the original decision.

But none of those things make a broken implementation better.

The correct response to a problem should be determined by the problem—not by how much we have already invested in the previous solution.

## Stop and ask

When something goes wrong, I increasingly try to ask:

1. **What exactly is the problem?**
2. **Is this actually the problem, or merely a symptom?**
3. **What are the possible solutions?**
4. **What does each solution cost?**
5. **How long until it produces a benefit?**
6. **What happens if the situation changes?**
7. **Am I choosing this because it works, or because everyone else is choosing it?**
8. **If I had not already invested in my current solution, would I choose it today?**

That last question is especially powerful.

## You don't have enough time to make every mistake yourself

There is a beautiful advantage to living after thousands of years of human history.

**Someone has probably already made the mistake you're about to make.**

You don't have to personally experience every failure.

You can study other people's failures.

Read history.

Read engineering postmortems.

Study businesses that collapsed.

Study infrastructure projects that went wrong.

Study financial bubbles.

Study failed products.

Study bad government programs.

Study your own previous mistakes.

Humanity has produced an enormous database of failure.

We should use it.

## Slow down

This isn't an argument for indecision.

Sometimes you have to act immediately.

If your house is burning, don't hold a committee meeting about the fire.

But most decisions aren't life or death.

For those decisions, taking ten minutes to step back can save years of regret.

The next time you feel the urge to immediately fix something, stop.

Take a breath.

Look at the actual problem.

And ask yourself:

> **"Is this worth it?"**

Then ask:

> **"Is this really the solution?"**

Sometimes the answer will be yes.

That's great.

But sometimes the answer will be no.

And realizing that **before** spending the money, time, and effort is one of the most valuable forms of efficiency.
